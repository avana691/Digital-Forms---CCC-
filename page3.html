The Generous City

We have become used to talking about cities as places under pressure. Housing is too expensive. Infrastructure is stretched. Loneliness is rising. Climate risks are no longer distant scenarios but lived reality. Migration is reshaping demography and politics. Inequality has moved from a side effect to one of the defining conditions cities must now navigate.

All of that is true. But it is also incomplete. Because cities are still one of the most powerful tools we have for society at large. They are where we organise everyday life. Where we build culture, institutions, infrastructure, markets, trust, conflict and care. They are where abstract systems become physical. Where energy becomes heat. Where policy becomes homes. Where climate becomes water in the street. Where inequality becomes distance to opportunity. Where loneliness becomes an empty apartment in a dense building.

That is why the future of cities cannot be treated as an urban niche. Cities are a societal tool. They shape how we live, how we produce, how we relate to each other and how we adapt to a world in disorder.

Last year, when speaking with politicians at Copenhagen City Hall, I used the framing of the generous city to describe this responsibility more clearly: a city that shows the way toward lower resource use, creates positive spillovers for the places around it and functions as infrastructure for innovation, not only through laboratories and start-ups, but through the everyday systems that make new practices possible.

A generous city is therefore not simply a nice city. It is a city that gives more than it takes. It creates value for the people who live in it, while also strengthening the places, systems and resources beyond its own boundaries. It reduces pressure on land, energy and materials. It supports the surrounding region rather than extracting from it. It makes culture, knowledge, infrastructure and innovation travel further than the city itself.

That is what makes thriving cities relevant at the scale of society. Thriving is not a soft aspiration, but a necessary urban capacity built through the cultural, physical and institutional conditions that allow knowledge to travel, people to belong, services to be shared, mobility to become less resource intensive and innovation to move from idea to practice.

Geoffrey West’s work on cities is useful here because it shows that cities are not only concentrations of people. They are systems of interaction. Proximity, density and exchange can increase the conditions for innovation, while allowing infrastructure, energy and services to be shared more efficiently. That is the promise. But the promise is not automatic. The same dynamics that make cities powerful can also intensify inequality, speculation, consumption and ecological pressure. In a world where seven of nine planetary boundaries are now assessed as breached, the question is no longer whether cities can grow. The question is whether they can become generous enough to create more value with fewer resources, more access with less exclusion, and more resilience without pushing the planet further out of balance.

The environmental case for the city is therefore both urgent and conditional. In theory, the city can be one of the most efficient forms of human settlement. Shared infrastructure, smaller homes, public transport, district heating, cycling, walking, repair, reuse, proximity and access can all reduce pressure on land, resources and energy. In practice, this only works when the city is designed, governed and financed to deliver it. A compact city filled with unaffordable housing, speculative vacancy, social fragmentation and high material consumption is not a sustainable city. It is only dense.

For the environmental case to move from theory to practice, co-responsibility becomes essential because the complex challenges cities face exceed the mandate, tools and incentives of any single actor. Municipalities can set direction, but they cannot carry the transformation alone. Markets can mobilise capital and delivery capacity, but only within conditions that reward long-term societal value. Citizens, researchers, developers, investors and housing providers all hold different forms of knowledge, power and risk, and the city only begins to work differently when those roles are made visible and aligned around more than the single project.

Co-responsibility is the practical architecture of a thriving city. It means that each actor brings their own legitimate interests into the open and aligns them around outcomes that are larger than the individual project. It requires clear direction, transparent interests, early involvement, shared risk and the ability to use the right resources at the right time. It is not about everyone doing the same. It is about everyone taking responsibility for more than their own part.

A changed economy needs a changed housing logic
The world in which cities operate has changed faster than our housing logic. Energy is geopolitical. Materials are strategic. Climate adaptation is unavoidable. Migration will shape the future of European cities. Inequality has been supersized beyond recognition, while mental health and loneliness have become part of the urban question. The economy as we used to know it is no more, and it is dangerously incomplete to believe that we can build our way out of the housing crisis by adding supply within the same logic that helped create the problem.

More homes are needed in many places. That should be stated clearly, because the point is not to dismiss supply. The point is that volume alone is not a strategy if what we build remains unaffordable, carbon-intensive, resource-heavy, poorly connected and unable to support the lives people actually need to live. The old assumption that prosperity will trickle through the system if we keep building, expanding and optimising belongs to a world that no longer exists. In the world we are now in, the housing question has to be connected to climate, resources, social cohesion, labour markets, mobility, public health and the long-term capacity of cities to function.

That is why housing has to be treated as infrastructure for the city and for society, rather than only as a market response to demand. Like energy, transport and digital systems, housing shapes the conditions for almost everything else. It determines whether people can work, care, study, rest, belong and contribute. It determines whether essential workers and middle-class workers can live in the city they help keep functioning, whether children grow up with stability or stress, and whether the city remains open as a place of opportunity and social mobility, or slowly closes around wealth. When housing prices exclude the very people who make the city work, inequality has been supersized into a structural urban condition. When housing no longer gives people access to opportunity, the city also weakens one of the conditions that drives innovation. When housing is reduced to a commodity, the city loses one of its most important tools for building long-term resilience.

The opportunity is that cities give us a way to respond to global disruption at a scale where change becomes tangible. Climate adaptation can be designed through nature, water, streets, buildings and everyday public space. Public and private capital can be aligned around long-term outcomes. Culture and social cohesion can become part of resilience. Housing can be planned, financed and governed as part of the wider urban system, where affordability, climate, mobility and everyday life are connected conditions for a city that works.

This does not make capital the enemy. Capital is necessary. The problem is misalignment. High-demand cities have real leverage because access to the city itself is valuable, and that leverage should be used more intelligently. Public land, planning rights, procurement, regulation, partnerships and investment models can all shape the conditions under which value is created. The question is whether that value is allowed to narrow around the individual asset, or whether it is directed toward outcomes that strengthen the city and the society around it.

The task is to build new financial alliances that share risk, unlock patient capital and make long-term urban value investable. That does not mean removing market actors from the equation. It means asking the market to deliver within a clearer societal frame, where affordability, climate, resilience, quality of life and innovation capacity are part of the value proposition from the beginning. In that sense, high-demand cities have a particular responsibility. They are not powerless recipients of market pressure. They can set direction if they are willing to use their position more deliberately.

This is also where co-responsibility becomes practical. The public sector cannot carry the transformation alone, and the private sector cannot define value only around the individual asset, project or actor. Developers, investors, municipalities, housing providers, researchers, civil society and citizens all hold different forms of knowledge, power and risk. Their interests are not the same, and they do not need to be. But they do need to be visible, negotiated and aligned around outcomes that deliver value for society. Good business has always had the potential to solve societal problems. We seem to have forgotten that, and cities are one of the places where we need to remember it because they are where societal problems become concrete enough to solve and close enough to connect to new forms of practice.

Generosity as urban value creation
This requires a richer understanding of resilience than the one often used in urban development. Resilience is still too often reduced to technical adaptation: flood protection, stormwater systems, energy backup and stronger buildings. All of that matters, and none of it can be ignored. But a resilient city also needs trust, memory, institutions, places where people meet and a social fabric strong enough to absorb shocks without breaking. It needs nature that cools, protects and restores. It needs infrastructure that works and communities that can respond.

The generous city of the future must therefore bring together challenges that are still too often treated separately: climate, affordability and mental health. It must adapt to heat, water, scarcity and migration. It must provide homes people can actually afford. And it must take loneliness seriously as a structural urban condition, rather than treating it only as a private feeling. A city can have the right technical systems and still fail people if it does not create the conditions for belonging, care and everyday dignity.

The future city will need to be rooted in democratic institutions, rights and public responsibility, while learning far more openly from places that have long dealt with scarcity, informality, density, climate extremes and community-based resilience. It will need to combine high technology with nature’s intelligence, financial sophistication with everyday care, and strategic planning with local improvisation. It will be shaped less by one model than by its ability to learn, adapt and connect knowledge across contexts.

To me, generosity can be an urban design principle for a world where extraction has reached its limits. A generous city gives more back to society than it consumes from it. It uses density to create access, capital to build long-term value, nature to protect and restore, culture to create belonging, creativity and meaning, governance to align interests and co-responsibility to make complexity productive. It creates value for the people who live in it, while also strengthening the places, systems and resources beyond its own boundaries.

It is also innovation infrastructure in the deepest sense: a place where societal problems become concrete enough to solve, close enough to connect across actors and important enough to move beyond the individual project. The generous city is one of the places where we can remember it, because it makes visible how value can be created for the individual, the organisation, the city and society at the same time.

That is the larger promise of the city. It can be one of the most powerful forms of local adaptation in a globally unstable world, but only if we ask more of it than growth, more of it than supply and more of it than technical optimisation. The city has always been one of humanity’s greatest inventions. In the century ahead, it may also become one of our most important forms of adaptation, if we are willing to build cities generous enough to let people, places and societies thrive.

After writing this, I became curious about who else had used the framing of the generous city. It turns out I am in very good company. Janine Benyus, the brilliant biologist and biomimicry thinker, used the term back in 2015 to describe a city that functions more like a healthy wild ecosystem: storing water, cooling the air, cleaning emissions and nurturing biodiversity. I naturally agree with all of that. It is just not quite the core of what I am trying to get at here. My version of the generous city starts from a related but slightly different place: the city as a societal tool that gives more than it takes, not only ecologically, but socially, economically, culturally and institutionally.

A note on supersized inequality
I use the phrase supersized inequality quite deliberately. The reference is Super Size Me, the film about McDonald’s and the logic of supersizing. The point is scale: the burger is still a burger, but at some point the size changes the consequences.

That is also how I think about inequality today. This is not the inequality we know from textbooks on economic theory, where differences in income, ownership and opportunity are treated as part of the ordinary distribution of a market economy. Supersized inequality describes a situation where the scale has shifted so dramatically that the category itself begins to break. When middle class workers and essential workers can no longer afford to live in the cities they keep functioning, inequality is no longer only a question of distribution. It has become a structural condition that changes what the city is able to be.

Stigmergy and the Memory of the City

Cities retain evidence of earlier use. A worn line through a lawn is the most familiar example, but a terrace someone set up on the pavement or a planning deviation someone was granted leaves a trace too, and each of these changes what the next actor sees as possible. A café owner puts chairs on the pavement, and passersby start treating pavement lingering as an accepted use of the street. A municipality approves one deviation from a planning rule, and the next applicant can point to that approval as a reason to expect the same outcome. In each case, an earlier action changes the physical or administrative setting the next actor encounters, and that changed setting stores part of the record of what happened before.

A useful term for describing this is stigmergy, introduced by the French zoologist Pierre-Paul Grassé in 1959 to describe how termites build their nests without a blueprint held by any single termite or by the colony as a whole. Workers deposit pheromone-laden soil pellets more or less at random across the site. Once one deposit reaches a critical size, it attracts further deposits through positive feedback, because the accumulated pellets emit more pheromone and so become more attractive than the scattered ones around them. Pillars begin to emerge from that self-reinforcing process, not from any individual termite’s plan.1 To an observer, the resulting nest looks centrally planned. No termite held a design for the whole structure.

Cities work differently. People negotiate prices, draft plans, and contest decisions in court. Institutions allocate rights, money, and authority. But part of urban change still follows a comparable logic: an actor leaves a trace, a later actor reads it, and that reading produces a new trace in turn. Cities learn through traces. And because the capacity to leave a trace is unevenly distributed, a visible pattern needs investigation before it can serve as the basis for policy.

The city as working memory
The clearest example is the desire path. A formal footpath takes a right angle, and a strip of bare earth cuts directly across the grass instead. The strip can mean several things: a destination the design missed, an inconvenient crossing, a shortcut that matters only when a school lets out. It can also reflect who made it. A wheelchair user cannot use a route a walker made by stepping over a kerb. A planner needs more than the strip itself before acting on it.

Other traces work the same way. A temporary terrace shows that part of a carriageway can support people sitting as well as vehicles moving through. Repeated complaints about one crossing give a traffic department a record of where pedestrians feel at risk. Recurring requests for the same planning deviation suggest the rule behind it may no longer fit how people use a street. Each of these lets actors coordinate across time without meeting: a later entrepreneur sees that temporary use worked before and repeats it, a planner discovers an exception has been granted six times and treats a seventh request differently.

Christensen documented a comparable pattern in construction work, following how a partially finished wall passes from one trade to the next: a carpenter frames it, an electrician runs cable through the frame, the carpenter closes it up, and a painter finishes the surface, with each worker reading the physical state the previous one left behind rather than waiting for a supervisor’s instruction.2 Christensen is careful to note that this reading is not a mechanical reflex. Each worker interprets the state of the wall through their own trade knowledge and experience. The existing city works on the same principle: it is both the object a project changes and the record that coordinates who does what next.

Creating traces deliberately
Some of the most useful urban traces come from interventions a municipality builds specifically to generate them.

In 2009, New York City’s Department of Transportation closed sections of Broadway at Times Square and Herald Square to motor traffic, using paint, planters, and movable furniture instead of permanent construction. It measured travel times on surrounding streets, counted pedestrians in the new space, and recorded safety incidents before and after. The temporary materials let officials watch how people used the street before committing public money to a permanent redesign. The plazas were made permanent starting in 2010, and the full reconstruction of Times Square was completed in 2017.

A well-designed pilot can reveal behaviour that drawings, interviews, and models cannot observe directly: people sitting crossing at different points, moving furniture, changing routes. That evidence still needs the conditions to be worth trusting. The pilot has to run long enough for routines to shift, its consequences have to be ones residents actually notice, and the department running it has to accept a result that argues against the design it already preferred. A few chairs placed for one afternoon mainly document the organised event and provide little evidence about changes in everyday routines.

The memory is selective, and government produces much of it
Visible traces attract attention because they seem direct: a worn strip, a full terrace, a permit file. But every trace requires an actor with the resources to make it, and a resident who lacks the time, money, or legal standing to act leaves nothing comparable behind, whatever they actually need.

Opening a terrace takes money for furniture and the confidence to deal with permitting staff. Winning a planning deviation takes money to commission the studies it requires. A resident with less free time, a weaker command of the local language, or an uncertain residency status is unlikely to produce either kind of record. Needs differ, too, in how visibly they surface: children without a play area play in a car park instead, and a neighbour who dislikes the noise may report them as a nuisance rather than as evidence of a missing facility. A resident who finds a street unsafe simply stops walking it after dark, and a traffic count taken there months later will show low use that a planner might read as low demand rather than fear.

The same limit applies to administrative traces. Six approved deviations from one rule might mean the rule is obsolete. They might just as easily mean one type of applicant, a developer with in-house legal staff, has repeatedly found the resources to escape it. Research on municipal 311 complaint systems finds that reporting behaviour tracks socioeconomic and demographic differences in residents’ propensity and ability to report, which means complaint data can under- or overstate the conditions it is meant to record.4 Cities record what actors with sufficient resources did. They record far less about what residents without those resources needed but could not act on.

Government adds a second layer to this selectivity. It is one of the largest producers of stigmergic signals in a city, and its traces do not behave like spontaneous ones. A temporary traffic measure can remain embedded in maps and policy documents long after its original justification has expired. An area classified as “vulnerable” in one report may keep attracting interventions designed for vulnerability, while activity that contradicts the label goes unrecorded because no survey asks about it. A permit granted once can become a reference point that later applicants invoke, even without formal legal weight. Through classifications, permits and temporary measures, government writes many of the traces it later treats as evidence. The institutional question is who holds the mandate to revisit a classification, retire a reference point, or end a measure that the system has started treating as settled. Better data and better pilots are process fixes for that question, not answers to it.

When traces become traps
Vacancy shows how the same mechanism can lock a city into decline. An empty building stops generating the traces that use produces, and graffiti, broken windows, and accumulated waste become the traces a passerby sees instead. Visible decline can raise perceived risk, which may discourage the investment that would fund repairs, and the absence of that investment lets the decline continue. The building keeps coordinating behaviour. It just now coordinates it against the reuse that would end the vacancy.

An early dominant use can lock in a different pattern. Once a street becomes known for nightlife or expensive restaurants, anyone considering that street tends to treat the existing use as the baseline they must fit into, and a business offering something different can face a higher bar than the businesses that established the street’s identity did, even when that identity came from a handful of early, largely accidental choices.

Erasing a trace has its own cost. A demolished building takes with it the evidence of how earlier occupants adapted the space. A replaced permit database makes past exceptions hard to retrieve. Staff who leave take informal project knowledge with them, because it existed only in memory, never in a written report.

Some provisions have to exist regardless of registered use. Accessibility, safety, and basic services cannot depend on users having produced enough of a trace to justify them, and a right does not become optional because no one has yet documented the need for it. The termite analogy helps explain how urban patterns accumulate. Questions about rights, safety, and basic provision still require political and legal judgement that no volume of observed behaviour can substitute for.

Reading traces as evidence
Municipalities already hold much of the relevant evidence, although it is divided between footfall counts, maintenance logs, permit files, vacancy registers, and complaints, each documenting a slice of how residents use the city. Alone, a record documents one incident. Compared across departments and years, the same records can reveal a pattern no single department noticed on its own. And a planner has to separate a trace from a preference: a busy route can show genuine convenience or a lack of alternatives. Repetition provides a reason to investigate; the appropriate response still requires judgement.

One way to reveal what existing traces conceal is to create what might be called a counter-trace: a condition a department deliberately creates to let behaviour the existing environment has suppressed become observable. Take an inaccessible route with a set of stairs where a ramp should be. A temporary ramp can reveal use that the stairs previously suppressed, without creating a new need. The wheelchair users who then appear on the route were already there; the stairs had simply kept them out of the data. A temporary shade structure or a free evening programme works on the same principle, revealing suppressed summer use or bringing out residents who never attend a daytime consultation because they work during the day.

That gives government an active role beyond observing whatever traces already exist. It can preserve a useful trace, investigate an ambiguous one, and design a counter-trace that lets a weak signal appear for the first time. It also keeps the responsibility to weigh competing claims and enforce a limit, something repeated informal use can never settle on its own. These tools can reduce the gap between recorded behaviour and unmet need. Some needs will still remain outside every municipal dataset, reaching the city only through a complaint or an election, or not reaching it at all.

Before paving the shortcut, extending the terrace or granting the seventh exception, a municipality should ask whose behaviour the existing environment has made visible, and whose needs its own rules have kept out of view.
